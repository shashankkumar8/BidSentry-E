Let me tell you something important: your problem probably isn't that you don't have access to good AI models. It's that you're using AI at the wrong stage of the problem-solving process.

You're doing what a huge number of hackathon participants are tempted to do:

Receive a problem statement (PS).

Paste it into ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or all three.

Ask for an innovative solution.

Pick an idea that sounds impressive.

Build something.

Present it and hope the judges like it.

The problem is that the models are usually pretty good at generating plausible ideas, but a plausible idea isn't necessarily a distinctive, useful, technically impressive hackathon project.

When everyone gives similar context to similar models and asks them the same question, they often get similar solution patterns: AI assistants, dashboards, chatbots, recommendation systems, prediction tools, and all-in-one platforms.

You end up competing with teams that have built a very similar idea, sometimes with better execution, deeper technical work, stronger evidence, or a better demonstration.

The solution isn't to stop using AI. It's to stop outsourcing your thinking to AI.

Use it to investigate the problem, challenge your assumptions, evaluate trade-offs, speed up implementation, and test your work. You should own the crucial decisions: which problem matters most, what opportunity other teams are missing, what to build, and why your solution deserves to win.

There is no process that can guarantee a hackathon victory. But there is a disciplined process that can substantially improve your odds of creating a shortlist-worthy project.

1. Understand what you're actually competing to win
A hackathon has at least four different challenges hidden inside it.

Problem selection

Find a specific, important problem rather than choosing the first idea that sounds exciting.

Differentiation

Find a valuable angle that isn't simply another version of the same solution everyone is building.

Execution

Build a working, coherent product that demonstrates the central idea convincingly within the available time.

Proof and communication

Make it easy for judges to understand why the problem matters, what is different, and what your product actually achieves.

These aren't theoretical concerns. For example, a 2026 ImpactHack rubric assigns separate weights to impact, technical execution, innovation, functionality, AI and technology use, and presentation. MLH's standard digital-event guidance instead gives equal weight to technology, design, completion, and learning. The exact criteria therefore depend on the event you enter.

Let's go through these properly.

Step 1 — Decode the problem statement and the judging criteria
This is where you should spend your first serious thinking session.

Don't interpret a problem statement as merely a request to build an application. Read it as a combination of:

A problem someone wants solved.

A set of constraints.

A set of sponsor or organizer priorities.

A set of criteria that judges will use to compare submissions.

Read the entire rules page, not just the headline. Look for required APIs, particular technologies, eligibility requirements, submission format, deadlines, theme alignment, and any sponsor-specific award tracks. Devpost's own planning guidance recommends checking requirements and judging criteria before brainstorming and comparing ideas on feasibility, time, and relevance.

For each criterion, write down the evidence you need. Don't just put "good UI" or "innovative AI."

For example:

Impact: What user problem does the product address? Can you demonstrate a meaningful improvement?

Technical execution: What technical challenge did you solve, and why did your architecture or method make sense?

Innovation: What does your approach do differently from existing alternatives?

Functionality: Can someone complete the central task in the demo without you manually intervening?

AI/technology: Would the solution materially lose its value if you removed the technology you're highlighting?

Presentation: Can a judge understand the product and its value in the first minute?

The last question for each criterion is: What would I have to show to earn a high score here?

This exercise changes your behavior. Instead of building ten features because they sound impressive, you start building evidence for the scores that matter.

Step 2 — Convert the PS into a precise problem definition
Most participants move from the problem statement straight to the solution.

Don't.

First, rewrite the PS in terms of a real user's experience.

Use this template:

User: Who specifically experiences the problem?

Situation: At what moment or in what context does it occur?

Current workaround: What do they do today?

Pain: What is slow, expensive, unreliable, inaccessible, risky, or frustrating?

Root cause: Why does the problem continue to exist?

Desired outcome: What should become measurably better?

Constraints: What limits the solution—time, connectivity, cost, data, hardware, regulation, or user behavior?

Unknowns: Which assumptions have not yet been verified?

The crucial distinction is between a symptom and a root cause.

For instance, imagine a PS about reducing food wastage.

"Restaurants waste food" describes a symptom.

Potential underlying problems include unpredictable demand, poor inventory visibility, inaccurate expiry tracking, unreliable pickup coordination, and a mismatch between surplus food and the organizations able to collect it.

Those are different problems requiring different solutions.

An application that tries to address all five will likely become too broad for a short hackathon. A project that solves one particularly painful bottleneck might be much more compelling.

Ask "why?" repeatedly until you can identify a bottleneck your team can reasonably address. Then form a hypothesis, rather than pretending you already know the answer.

Example:

We believe small restaurants waste sellable meals because staff discover the surplus too late for nearby recipient organizations to arrange pickup. If we make surplus availability actionable early enough, more food could be collected before it becomes unusable.

Notice that this is a hypothesis to test, not a claim that you have established the cause.

That distinction matters: a hackathon idea becomes stronger when its important assumptions are investigated rather than merely described confidently.

Step 3 — Investigate what already exists
This is one of the most valuable steps that generic AI brainstorming skips or performs superficially.

Before generating solutions, investigate existing approaches.

Search for:

Commercial products addressing the problem.

Open-source GitHub repositories.

Previous submissions to the same or related hackathons.

Research papers, public datasets, and relevant APIs.

Failed approaches, recurring complaints, and workflow limitations.

Users discussing their existing workarounds in forums or communities.

Your aim is not to prove that nobody has ever built anything similar. That is rarely a realistic bar.

Your aim is to understand what already works, what does not work well enough, and where you can provide a meaningful improvement.

Look beyond other hackathon projects, too. A project may look original compared with last year's winners yet duplicate an existing commercial product.

The most important question to ask
What do existing solutions make the user do that they shouldn't have to do?

This often reveals stronger ideas than asking what feature you could add.

Maybe a user still needs to verify every AI-generated result manually. Maybe the information arrives too late. Perhaps a tool gives a prediction but no actionable next step. Maybe a solution assumes reliable internet access or requires users to enter information that already exists elsewhere.

Each of these is a possible innovation opportunity—but only after you establish that it is relevant to the target user.

Step 4 — Find the wedge that differentiates your solution
This is the most important thinking stage if your current projects feel too similar to everyone else's.

A common mistake is assuming that innovation means inventing an entirely new category of product. It doesn't have to.

You can differentiate by choosing a much narrower problem, a neglected user group, a better workflow, a different technical approach, a useful integration, better evidence, or a substantially more reliable result.

A good hackathon project usually has a focused starting point rather than trying to become a complete platform immediately.

Think of the core opportunity as:

A specific user + a high-friction task + a better method + a demonstrable outcome.

Consider an illustrative education problem.

GENERIC APPROACH

AI exam preparation assistant

Upload notes, generate a summary, create quizzes, and chat with a tutor.

MORE SPECIFIC HYPOTHESIS

A prerequisite-gap detector for a specific exam syllabus

A learner answers a short diagnostic quiz. The tool identifies which prerequisite concepts may be causing repeated mistakes, links each diagnosis to supporting answers, and creates a focused revision sequence using the actual syllabus and past papers.

The second idea is not automatically original—similar products may exist, and that must be researched. But it gives you a much sharper hypothesis to investigate.

It also leads to a more meaningful demo: show the learner's answer, identify the underlying gap, trace the evidence, and generate the next study action. You can demonstrate the complete reasoning workflow rather than displaying a generic chatbot response.

Here are five useful ways to search for that kind of wedge:

1. Narrow the user. Don't target everybody affected by the issue. Find a group with a distinctive need or constraint.

2. Narrow the moment. Identify the exact point in a workflow where the existing process fails.

3. Remove a handoff. Can your system turn information into an action without unnecessary copying, searching, or coordination?

4. Make an unreliable task verifiable. Could evidence, structured outputs, evaluation, or human review make an existing tool more trustworthy?

5. Solve an overlooked constraint. Consider language, connectivity, cost, accessibility, integration, latency, or limited data.

The best option is the one that addresses a genuine problem and can be meaningfully demonstrated within your time and resources. A clever twist that doesn't matter to the user is not an advantage.

3. How to use ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini without getting generic ideas
Here's the change I would make immediately.

Stop asking three models to solve the same problem. Assign them different jobs in a problem-solving process.

When you ask all three, "Give me the most innovative solution for this PS," you're still asking the same fundamental question. Getting three answers doesn't necessarily produce three independent, well-researched opportunities.

Instead, use AI in five distinct roles.

One rule that makes these prompts more effective
Do not let the models choose your idea through majority voting.

Use different models to expose different perspectives, but choose based on evidence, constraints, and the judging rubric. Three models agreeing that an idea sounds good is not the same as three independent users confirming the problem exists.

Also, don't make the models the sole source of novelty. Your own observations from studying existing products, talking to people, and testing the difficult parts of a workflow are where useful differentiation is more likely to emerge.

Scores are only as good as the evidence behind them. A high total cannot compensate for a fatal dependency, an ineligible idea, or a technically impossible core feature.

A few additional rules help:

Reject ideas that violate eligibility requirements, even if everything else is attractive.

Investigate any low feasibility or data-readiness score before committing.

Prefer a specific idea with one strong differentiator over a broad idea with ten conventional features.

If two ideas score similarly, test the riskiest assumption before choosing.

Your goal is not to select the idea with the most features. It is to select the idea with the strongest combination of value, differentiation, feasibility, and demonstrable proof.

5. Validate the idea before building the product
A high-risk mistake is spending 12 hours implementing an idea and discovering that the most important part doesn't work.

Before you commit, run a small validation sprint.

You do not need a full market study. You need enough evidence to make a better decision than blind guessing.

The three kinds of validation
A. Problem validation

Try to establish whether the user's problem is real.

Talk to a few representative users if possible. Ask what they actually do today, when the problem occurred most recently, what happened as a result, and what they have already tried.

Avoid leading questions such as "Would you use my amazing AI app?" People may say yes to be polite.

Better questions include:

"Show me how you currently handle this."

"Which part takes the most effort?"

"What happens when this goes wrong?"

"Have you tried another tool? Why did you stop using it?"

For a short hackathon, even three to five relevant conversations can uncover useful assumptions. Treat such a small sample as directional evidence, not proof that an entire market wants your product.

When direct conversations are impossible, use credible research, documented complaints, existing workflows, and public data. Be honest about what you have and haven't validated.

B. Solution validation

Create the simplest artifact that lets someone react to the actual mechanism: perhaps a clickable prototype, a mock result generated from a realistic input, or a minimal functional workflow.

Observe whether the proposed solution actually addresses the problem you identified.

C. Technical validation

Test the most uncertain technical dependency before building the rest of the product.

Examples:

Can the required API return the fields you need?

Is suitable data available and legally usable?

Can your approach achieve acceptable latency?

Can a model handle the difficult inputs, not just the obvious example?

Can an unreliable external service be replaced with a safe fallback?

A useful rule: test the assumption that could kill the project before implementing the parts that depend on it.

6. Design an MVP that judges can remember
Here is another reason projects fail to stand out: teams build a large collection of features instead of one complete, impressive workflow.

Think in terms of a vertical slice: a small portion of the product that works from beginning to end.

For example:

1. User supplies input

An actual task or realistic test case

2. Your core mechanism works

The analysis, algorithm, integration, or AI process that makes your idea different

3. A usable result appears

Relevant, understandable, and verifiable

4. The user can act

The workflow produces a useful next step or outcome

Your minimum viable product should complete this central journey reliably.

For the first milestone, it is often worth sacrificing secondary features, advanced settings, elaborate onboarding, extra user roles, and complex analytics to make the main journey work well.

Find your one "wow moment"
I recommend that every team answer this question:

What is the one thing we can show in our demo that makes the judge think, "That's genuinely useful or technically interesting"?

Possible examples include:

A difficult task completed in a single workflow instead of several manual steps.

A useful output produced from a messy real-world input.

A technical method that works under a constraint competing tools don't handle well.

An explanation that shows why a recommendation was made and where the evidence came from.

A reliable end-to-end process that connects two systems which normally leave the user doing the work manually.

A visual demonstration of a meaningful improvement, supported by a real measurement.

Do not try to manufacture a spectacular feature merely for the demo. The wow moment should be the natural consequence of solving the chosen problem well.

Don't build an AI wrapper and mistake it for a complete solution
Using an LLM API is not inherently unoriginal. The issue is when the application adds little beyond a prompt box and the resulting text.

Consider what your application contributes around the model:

Is there specialised input preparation?

Is there relevant contextual data?

Does it connect to a real workflow?

Can it verify outputs?

Does it remember or act on structured state?

Is there an evaluation method?

Can it recover safely when the model is wrong?

Does it produce an outcome that a general-purpose assistant doesn't provide as effectively?

If you can't explain what your product adds beyond access to the underlying model, keep investigating.

Make technical quality visible
You don't necessarily need the largest model, the most complex architecture, or the most lines of code. You need technology that supports your claim.

For AI-heavy projects, consider preparing a small evaluation set of representative inputs, including edge cases. Check whether the answers are correct, useful, consistent, and safe for the intended use. Where appropriate, track latency, cost, source traceability, and failure rates.

If your application produces recommendations, predictions, or generated content, determine how you will establish that the result is useful rather than merely plausible.

And never invent benchmark results or present manually prepared outcomes as automatic system behavior. If you use demonstration data, label it clearly.

7. Manage the hackathon clock like a product team
You can have a promising idea and still fail because you spend too long thinking, start integrating too late, or discover at the final hour that the demo doesn't work.

For a 24-hour hackathon, I would start with the following budget.

Decode and investigate

2 hours
Understand the rubric, identify the user problem, and research alternatives.

Choose and scope

1 hour
Select one approach, validate its largest risk, and define the demo.

Build the core workflow

13 hours
Get an end-to-end path working early, then improve its functionality.

Integrate and test

3 hours
Test realistic cases, fix failures, verify integrations, and deploy.

Polish the demo and submission

3 hours
Prepare the narrative, README, video, screenshots, and final checks.

Contingency

2 hours
Leave room for unexpected technical problems or submission issues.

This is an example schedule, not a rigid formula. Adjust it for a 12-hour sprint, a 48-hour event, a longer innovation challenge, or a specialized hardware competition.

The principles are more important than the exact hours:

First third: You should have a selected idea, a defined scope, and evidence that the hardest dependency is feasible.

Middle third: The central user journey should work end-to-end, even if the UI is rough.

Final third: Improve reliability, complete the submission, and rehearse the demo. Avoid adding major new features.

A useful hard rule is to freeze new features once roughly 80% of the available time has elapsed, unless a missing feature prevents the core workflow from working.

Start the demo story before coding is complete
Write a rough demo script at the beginning of the build. This forces you to think about the user input, the product's central mechanism, and the result you want to show.

Keep refining it as you implement the real product.

8. Make the demo tell a convincing story
A project is not judged solely by what exists in the repository. Judges need to understand the work, and the easiest way to communicate that is to demonstrate the product solving the problem.

Devpost's guidance for hackathon demos recommends quickly establishing the problem, showing the working project rather than spending the time on mundane setup steps, and then explaining the potential impact.

For a short pitch, I would use this structure.

0:00–0:20
Open with the problem

Establish the target user, the real-world situation, and what currently goes wrong. Avoid a long introduction about your team or the size of the market.

0:20–0:40
Explain the insight

What did you identify that led to your approach? Why does the existing workflow leave an important gap?

0:40–1:40
Demonstrate the working product

Show a realistic input, the distinctive mechanism, and the resulting action or outcome. This is the centerpiece of the pitch.

1:40–2:10
Prove the value and technical substance

Explain how the product works, what you've actually tested, and which technical decision makes a difference.

2:10–2:30
Close with the outcome

State why this approach matters, the limitations that remain, and the most credible next step.

This is a sample 2.5-minute structure. Adapt it to the actual presentation length and rubric.

What not to do during the demo
Do not spend the opening minute describing every technology you used. Don't show your login flow, navigate through five menus, or explain the entire architecture before showing the product.

Don't start with a lengthy list of features. And don't rely on a slide containing a screenshot of a feature that doesn't actually work when the judge tries it.

Prepare one repeatable demonstration path. Use realistic test inputs, put required files and credentials in place beforehand, and keep a short screen-recorded demo as a fallback when the rules permit it.

Above all, show the outcome before explaining every implementation detail.

Expect judges to ask these questions
Practice answering the following without becoming defensive:

What is different about this compared with existing tools?

Who specifically needs this?

How do you know the problem matters?

What does the AI, algorithm, or integration actually contribute?

What was the hardest technical challenge?

What evidence shows that the result is correct or useful?

What would you improve with another week?

Which parts work today, and which are still planned?

Honest, specific answers are stronger than claiming that an early prototype is a complete production-ready platform.

1. Why AI coding tools keep giving you mediocre projects
When you tell an AI coding agent, "Build this hackathon project from scratch," it has to make dozens of decisions you haven't specified: product structure, user flows, visual design, data models, backend logic, validation, loading states, error handling, testing, and deployment.

Unless you guide those decisions, it tends to choose a path that looks complete quickly rather than one that satisfies the deeper goal you have in mind.

You end up with a website that has a landing page, a dashboard, a few buttons, maybe an API integration, and some fake or hardcoded data. It looks like a project, but the important workflows are shallow or incomplete.

There are five distinct problems here.

1. File generation is mistaken for implementation

The agent creates components, routes, and utility files, but the components don't form a coherent working product.

2. The interface has no deliberate design direction

You get a generic navbar, cards, gradients, dashboard, and buttons rather than a distinctive interface designed for a specific user and task.

3. Everything is built at once

The agent spreads effort across ten features before proving that even one important workflow works end-to-end.

4. Real functionality is replaced with appearances

Forms accept input but don't persist it. Charts display sample numbers. Buttons do nothing. AI results aren't validated. Authentication or integrations are simulated without being labelled as simulations.

5. Nothing forces the agent to prove that it finished

The agent says the build is complete without demonstrating that the app starts, the major flows work, and the deployed result matches the intended design.

This isn't simply a model-quality issue. It's also a workflow issue.

For example, Cursor's current documentation specifically recommends Plan Mode for complex tasks, with a reviewable implementation plan before code is written. Those capabilities are valuable because they close the gap between generating code and verifying a working product. (Cursor Plan Mode, Cursor Browser)

Your new rule should be: never measure progress by the number of generated files. Measure it by the number of important user tasks that work and have been tested.

Example: turning a vague feature into a working feature
Say your project includes uploading a CSV file and detecting anomalies.

A weak task is:

6. How to avoid the "random files and ZIP" problem specifically
Let's address your original complaint directly.

Which user workflows it verified.

8. The complete workflow I would use for your next hackathon
Here's how I would combine the problem-solving roadmap from your earlier question with this engineering process.

Phase A — Find the opportunity
Decode the PS and judging rubric.
Research actual users, current solutions, and underserved needs.
Generate materially different hypotheses and challenge your preferred one.
Select an idea using evidence, feasibility, and rubric fit.
Phase B — Define the product
Write the product specification and measurable acceptance criteria.
Define the smallest complete user journey and core differentiator.
Approve the architecture and visual direction.
Set up the repository, persistent rules, and implementation milestones.
Phase C — Build the product
Test the riskiest technical dependency early.
Implement one vertical slice at a time in the existing codebase.
Run the application and verify each milestone before expanding scope.
Use AI for implementation, debugging, testing, and code review—not just code generation.
Phase D — Make it competitive
Run functional and visual QA against the specification.
Fix critical defects and refine the main workflow.
Deploy, verify the live app, and prepare a fallback demo.
Submit with a clear explanation of the problem, differentiation, technical work, and evidence.
My final recommendation
For your next project, change just three habits first.

First: Never start implementation with a single "build the entire project" prompt. Start with a specification and an approved plan.

Second: Never move on from the core workflow until you have run it and verified that it works. Build breadth only after you have built depth.

Third: Never accept "done" as a test result. Make the agent show the actual commands, tests, browser checks, and outstanding limitations.

That combination will help you avoid much of the generic, shallow output you're getting today.

One last point: a polished app is not automatically a shortlist-worthy hackathon project. You need both a distinctive, well-supported solution and strong execution. The ideal workflow therefore doesn't merely produce a beautiful website—it produces a product whose central claim is real, whose important functionality can be demonstrated, and whose implementation supports the reason you believe it deserves to win.
